The Part of Meditation
Nobody Talks About

ManilaJuly 20268 min read
MeditationEmotionsSelf-Awareness
Back to Insights

We often begin meditation because we want to feel peaceful.

We want a quieter mind. Less anxiety. Better focus. Emotional balance. Perhaps even a few minutes where we don't have to think about everything that is happening around us. But nobody really tells you that meditation can feel quite the opposite in the beginning. You sit down, close your eyes and suddenly realise how much noise there is inside your own mind. Thoughts appear one after another. Conversations replay themselves. Tomorrow's worries show up. Something you haven't thought about in years suddenly comes back. The body feels uncomfortable. 

"And then another thought appears: "

“Am I even doing this right?”

I have experienced this myself.

And I have learnt that these experiences are not necessarily signs that meditation isn't working. Sometimes, they are the beginning of becoming aware.

When the mind refuses to be quiet: 

One of the first things meditation can show us is how restless the mind actually is. We are so used to constantly doing something—working, scrolling, talking, planning, solving that simply sitting still can feel unfamiliar. The moment we stop distracting ourselves, the mind gets louder. The answer isn't to fight every thought. It is to notice it. A thought comes. You notice it. You return to the breath. Another thought comes. You notice that too. You return again. 

Meditation isn't necessarily about having no thoughts.

It is about slowly changing our relationship with them.

When the body becomes uncomfortable:

Sitting quietly can also make us suddenly aware of the body. The back hurts. The legs feel restless. The neck feels uncomfortable. We want to move. And sometimes we become so focused on trying to maintain the “perfect” meditation posture that meditation itself becomes another task we have to perform correctly. It doesn't have to be that way. Meditation doesn't require us to punish the body. A comfortable chair can work. A cushion can work. Lying down can work in certain practices.

The posture matters far less than the quality of awareness we bring to the practice.

When emotions begin to surface:

This is perhaps the part of meditation that deserves more attention.

When we become quiet, we may become aware of things that were easier to avoid when life was constantly busy.

Sadness.

Anger.

Fear.

Loneliness.

Old memories.

Unfinished emotions.

This can sometimes feel unsettling.

But becoming aware of an emotion doesn't mean that something has gone wrong. Awareness simply allows us to see what is already there.

At the same time, meditation isn't a requirement to sit alone with overwhelming emotions. If a practice brings up intense or distressing experiences, seeking appropriate professional support can be an important part of the process.

Healing is not about forcing ourselves to face everything at once.

Sometimes, healing is learning how to meet ourselves gently.

When consistency becomes difficult” 

There will be days when you don't want to meditate.

There will be busy mornings, late nights, distracted days and periods when the practice simply disappears from your routine.

That doesn't mean you have failed.

I have always felt that consistency is less about never breaking the practice and more about returning to it.

Five mindful minutes can be more meaningful than an hour spent feeling pressured to meditate.

The practice becomes sustainable when it becomes a part of your life rather than another responsibility on your list.

When we expect something to happen: 

Perhaps the biggest challenge is expectation. We expect meditation to make us calm. We expect it to remove our anxiety. We expect to feel spiritually connected. We expect our thoughts to disappear. And when none of that happens, we wonder whether meditation is working. But meditation isn't a performance, and there isn't always an immediate result to measure. 

Some days you may feel peaceful.

Some days you may feel restless.

Some days you may notice something about yourself that you had been avoiding.

And some days, nothing particularly remarkable may happen at all.

That is okay.

The value of meditation may not always be found in what we feel during those few minutes.

Sometimes, we notice its influence much later—in the pause before reacting, in the ability to sit with an uncomfortable thought, in becoming aware of an emotion before it takes over, or simply in feeling a little more present in our own lives.

Perhaps meditation is not about becoming someone else\!

I don't think meditation is about becoming a person who never gets anxious, never overthinks, never gets angry or never feels emotionally overwhelmed.

We are human. The mind will wander. Life will hurt. Emotions will come and go. Perhaps the deeper practice is learning to remain connected to ourselves through all of it.

To observe without immediately judging.

A gentle reminder

To pause before reacting.

To listen before escaping.

To become familiar with our own inner world.

And slowly, with practice, to create a little more space between what we experience and how we choose to respond. That space can become a very powerful place. And perhaps that is the part of meditation nobody talks about.

It doesn't always begin with peace.

Sometimes, it begins with finally becoming aware of everything that has been keeping us from it.
