**Why Do I Want to Go Back to the Person Who Has Hurt Me So Many Times?**

&nbsp;

There is a particular kind of confusion that can happen after a painful relationship ends.

You know what happened.

You remember the disrespect.

You remember the lies, the inconsistency, the arguments, the disappointment.

You remember how many times you promised yourself that you would not go back.

And yet, when that person is no longer there, you miss them.

You want to reach out.

You start remembering the good parts.

You wonder whether things could be different this time.

And then comes the question:

**“If this person has hurt me so many times, why do I still want them?”**

This question is often accompanied by self-judgment.

*Maybe I am weak.*

*Maybe I don't have enough self-respect.*

*Maybe I am addicted to them.*

But human attachment is much more complicated than that.

Wanting someone does not automatically mean that the relationship is good for us.

And knowing that someone is bad for us does not automatically switch off our attachment to them.

Both can exist at the same time.

**Attachment doesn't disappear just because a relationship becomes painful**

When we become emotionally attached to someone, the relationship becomes part of our psychological world.

Their presence, messages, affection, attention and even their absence can begin to influence our emotional state.

So when the relationship ends, we are not simply losing a person.

We may also be losing a familiar emotional structure.

The routines.

The conversations.

The anticipation.

The feeling of being connected to someone.

Even the conflicts can become familiar.

This is why intellectually knowing *“this relationship hurts me”* may not be enough to stop emotionally wanting the person.

The cognitive understanding and the attachment system do not always move at the same speed.

**Intermittent reinforcement can make attachment particularly difficult**

One psychological mechanism that can help explain some of these relationships is **intermittent reinforcement**.

When affection, attention or reassurance are unpredictablesometimes present and sometimes withdrawnthey can become especially psychologically salient.

You don't know when the next moment of closeness will come.

And because it is unpredictable, you may find yourself paying even more attention to the person's behaviour.

A loving message after days of distance can feel disproportionately powerful.

An apology after repeated conflict can bring enormous relief.

A sudden return after abandonment can feel like proof that the connection still matters.

This doesn't mean that every inconsistent relationship creates a psychological dependency.

But unpredictable reinforcement can make a pattern particularly difficult to disengage from.

You may find yourself waiting for the *good version* of the relationship to return.

**Sometimes we become attached to the possibility, not the reality**

This is one of the most painful parts.

You may not actually be longing for the relationship exactly as it was.

You may be longing for what you believed it could become.

You remember the beginning.

The potential.

The moments when they were caring.

The conversations about the future.

The version of them that appeared occasionally and made you believe that everything could eventually work.

So the mind starts negotiating with reality.

*“Maybe they have changed.”*

*“Maybe I reacted too strongly.”*

*“Maybe if we communicate better…”*

*“Maybe this time…”*

Hope can keep us psychologically attached to a possibility even when the repeated evidence is telling us something different.

**Cognitive dissonance makes letting go harder**

There is another psychological process worth understanding: **cognitive dissonance**.

When we have invested significant time, emotion, hope and identity into a relationship, accepting that it may not be what we believed it was can be painful.

The mind doesn't like contradictions.

*“I loved this person deeply”* and *“this person repeatedly hurt me”* are difficult truths to hold together.

Sometimes, the mind tries to reduce that discomfort by changing the story.

*Maybe it wasn't that bad.*

*Maybe I misunderstood.*

*Maybe they didn't mean it.*

*Maybe I should give them another chance.*

This doesn't necessarily mean we are consciously deceiving ourselves.

It can simply be the mind trying to create coherence between our emotional investment and what actually happened.

**Familiarity can be mistaken for compatibility**

There is also something quieter happening.

We often become comfortable with what we know.

That doesn't mean we consciously choose pain.

It means that familiar emotional patterns require less psychological uncertainty than unfamiliar ones.

After leaving a relationship, healthy stability can initially feel strangely unfamiliar.

There may be no dramatic highs and lows.

No waiting for a message.

No wondering where you stand.

No intense reconciliation after conflict.

And sometimes, a nervous system accustomed to unpredictability can experience calm as unfamiliar rather than immediately comforting.

This is one reason that *“I feel such a strong connection with them”* is not always enough evidence that the relationship is right for us.

Intensity and compatibility are not the same thing.

**Sometimes the longing is for validation**

There can also be a deeper psychological need underneath the desire to return.

Sometimes we want the person who hurt us to finally become the person who makes us feel worthy.

We want them to choose us.

To understand us.

To apologise.

To acknowledge what they did.

To finally give us the love we were asking for.

And this can create a painful psychological loop:

**The person who caused the wound becomes the person from whom we seek relief from that wound.**

When that happens, returning may feel like an attempt to resolve something emotionally unfinished.

But another person's validation cannot permanently settle our sense of worth.

**Missing someone is not evidence that you should return**

This distinction is important.

You can miss someone and still know that being with them is not good for you.

You can love someone and recognise that the relationship is unhealthy.

You can remember beautiful moments without forgetting the painful ones.

You can feel tempted to reach out without acting on that impulse.

And you can grieve someone who was not good for you.

**Emotions are information. They are not always instructions.**

Just because you feel something strongly does not mean you have to act on it.

**So, what should I ask myself?**

Instead of only asking:

**“Why do I still want this person?”**

perhaps ask:

**“What exactly am I longing for?”**

Is it the person?

The familiarity?

The affection?

The validation?

The hope that they will change?

The beginning of the relationship?

The fear of being alone?

The relief that comes when they come back?

Or the feeling that this time, you might finally get the ending you wanted?

These questions don't tell you what decision to make.

They help you understand the psychological need underneath the decision.

And understanding that need gives you more choice.

**Perhaps this is where healing begins**

I don't think healing always begins with *“I need to stop loving this person.”*

Sometimes it begins with something much more honest:

**“I need to understand why I am attached to this person.”**

Because once we understand the pattern, we can begin to separate different things that have become mixed together:

Love from dependency.

Longing from compatibility.

Familiarity from safety.

Intensity from intimacy.

Hope from evidence.

And attachment from choice.

That separation can create clarity.

And clarity creates choice.

The person may still matter to you.

You may still miss them.

You may even still love them.

But slowly, the question can change from:

**“How do I make this person stay?”**

to:

**“What kind of relationship do I want to participate in?”**

And perhaps that is a much more important question.

Because healing is not always about becoming indifferent to the person who hurt you.

Sometimes, it is about becoming clear enough to stop abandoning yourself in order to keep the connection.

**Understanding is where healing begins.**
